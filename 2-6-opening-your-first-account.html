Myth Or Fact · 10 / 13

Myth Or Fact?

Eight statements about opening an account — some true, some the exact thing people get wrong. Tap your answer on each, in any order, and the reasoning appears underneath.

You need thousands of dollars to open your first investing account.

Myth. Most major providers now have no account minimum at all, and fractional shares mean a small deposit can still be invested rather than sitting idle.

Providers are legally required to verify your identity before you can invest.

Fact. Federal "know your customer" rules apply to every US brokerage. That's why the form asks for your SSN and date of birth, and it isn't something a provider can waive.

Once you link your bank account, you can only deposit — never withdraw.

Myth. An ACH link runs both directions. Selling first, and waiting for the sale to settle, is what adds time — not the link itself.

Money you deposit is automatically invested the moment it arrives.

Myth. By default a deposit lands as cash and stays there. Buying is a separate action, and plenty of accounts sit funded but uninvested for months because nobody realised.

SIPC protection covers you if your investments lose value.

Myth. SIPC steps in if the brokerage itself fails, working to restore your securities and cash up to its limits. A holding that simply drops in price is not covered by anything — that's ordinary market risk.

Opening a standard brokerage account runs a hard credit check that lowers your score.

Myth. A cash brokerage account normally uses a soft identity check, which doesn't affect your score. Applying for margin or a linked credit product is a different process with different rules.

You can hold accounts at more than one brokerage at the same time.

Fact. There's no limit, and transferring holdings between brokerages is a standard process. Some IRS contribution limits apply across all your accounts combined rather than per account.

The beneficiary you name on the account generally overrides what your will says.

Fact. A beneficiary designation usually controls, which is why an outdated one causes real problems. It's worth revisiting after a marriage, divorce, or death in the family.

Recap · 12 / 13

Here's What You Now Know

The whole lesson, in six lines.

✓Six pieces of information cover almost every application: SSN, photo ID, bank numbers, employment, address, and a beneficiary.
✓You choose an account type before you choose an investment — the wrapper sets the tax and withdrawal rules, not what's inside it.
✓Identity verification is required by law, uses a soft check that doesn't touch your credit score, and a hold is usually a typo rather than a rejection.
✓Linking a bank builds a two-way ACH pipe — instantly by logging in, or over a few days using micro-deposits.
✓Depositing and investing are two separate actions. Money lands as cash and stays there until you place an order.
✓An order ticket asks four things: ticker, amount, order type, and time in force — and a recurring transfer doesn't always buy anything on its own.
Your next move: open the sign-up page of a provider you're curious about and read the real form without submitting it. Seeing the actual fields is the fastest way to make the whole thing feel ordinary.
