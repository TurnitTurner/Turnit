Same Fund. Same Decade. Same $12,000.

One pays in the same amount every year. One pays in more after a good year and goes quiet after a bad one — performance chasing, in miniature. The fund's own time-weighted return cannot tell them apart; their dollar-weighted return can. Tap through all three.

Myth Or Fact · 5 / 8

Myth Or Fact?

Five claims. Tap your answer.

The return on your fund's page is the return you got.

Myth. That is the time-weighted return, and it assumes one lump sum sat there untouched for the whole period. If you paid in monthly — and almost everyone does — your own number is different, sometimes by a lot.

Your own return can be higher than the fund's.

Fact. It happens whenever more of your money happened to be invested through the good stretches — which steady monthly contributions into a market that dips halfway through will do for you automatically. The gap runs both ways.

Comparing your portfolio to the S&P 500 is the standard test.

Myth. It is the standard test for a portfolio of US large-company stocks. Measuring anything else against it — a mixed portfolio, a global one, one holding bonds — tells you about the difference in what you hold, not about how well you did.

Simple all-in-one funds tend to have narrower gaps than complex ones.

Fact. Morningstar finds the widest gaps in the most specialised categories and some of the narrowest in plain, broad ones. A fund that gives you nothing to react to is a fund you are less likely to mistime.

A single year against the right benchmark tells you if your plan is working.

Myth. One year is mostly noise even with a perfect yardstick. The useful version is the same comparison repeated over several years, where a persistent shortfall starts to mean something a single year never could.

Your Call · 6 / 8

What Should They Measure Against?

Three portfolios. Pick the benchmark that would actually tell them something about their own dollar-weighted return.

Everything in one US total-market index fund.

A US total-market index. Match the yardstick to what is actually held. The S&P 500 is close but excludes smaller companies, so any difference would be telling you about those, not about the investor.

70% global stocks, 30% bonds, rebalanced yearly.

The blended one. A mixed portfolio needs a mixed yardstick, built in the same proportions. Measured against stocks alone it will look bad in every rising year and heroic in every falling one, which is just the bonds talking.

Cash being saved for a house deposit in 18 months.

A savings or money-market rate. The job of this money is to still be there in eighteen months. Comparing it to stocks would only tempt you into risking a deposit you cannot afford to lose — the right benchmark reflects the goal, not the ambition.

Recap · 7 / 8

Here's What You Now Know

The whole lesson, in six lines.

✓The number on a fund's page is its time-weighted return, and it assumes a lump sum nobody added to.
✓Your dollar-weighted return counts when every dollar arrived, and it is the only one that paid for anything.
✓The behavior gap between the two ran 1.2 points a year across the whole US fund industry over the last decade.
✓The gap cuts both ways: paying in steadily through a dip can put you ahead of the fund's own figure, while performance chasing costs you the same gap in reverse.
✓A benchmark must match what you actually hold, which for a mixed portfolio means a blended benchmark.
✓One year against any yardstick is noise. A persistent shortfall — the same comparison repeated, year after year — is the signal.
Your next move: find your own dollar-weighted return — most brokerages show it as "personal rate of return" or "your return", separately from the fund's. Compare the two.
