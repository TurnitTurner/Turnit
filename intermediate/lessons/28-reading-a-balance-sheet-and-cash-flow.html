Myth Or Fact · 5 / 8

Myth Or Fact?

Five claims. Tap your answer.

A profitable company can run out of cash.

Fact. Profit counts a sale when it is made; cash arrives when the customer pays. A company growing fast enough can book record profits and still be unable to make payroll. It is one of the most common ways a good business dies.

High equity means the company is worth that much.

Myth. Equity is an accounting leftover built from what assets cost, not what they are worth now. A brand built over decades may be worth a fortune and appear nowhere on the balance sheet.

Cash leaving under financing can be a good sign.

Fact. Money going out under financing is often dividends paid and debt repaid — a mature company handing cash back because it has more than it can usefully invest. Money coming in there can mean the opposite.

Debt on the balance sheet is a warning sign.

Myth. Borrowing cheaply to build something that earns more than it costs is ordinary business. What matters is whether operating cash flow comfortably covers the interest, not the size of the number.

The balance sheet is a snapshot, not a period.

Fact. It describes one single day. The income statement and cash flow statement both cover a stretch of time. That is why a balance sheet can be tidied up shortly before the date it is struck.

Which One · 6 / 8

Where Would You Look?

Four questions. Which statement answers each one?

How much does it owe, and how soon?

Balance sheet. It lists every claim against the company and splits them into what falls due within a year and what falls due later.

Did last year's profit turn into real money?

Cash flow. Compare operating cash flow with net income. If profit keeps climbing while cash does not follow it, that gap is the first thing to ask about.

What would be left for shareholders if it wound up?

Balance sheet. That is equity, in theory. In practice the assets would rarely fetch their stated value, so treat the figure as a starting point rather than a payout.

Is it funding itself, or raising money to survive?

Cash flow. A company whose operations throw off cash is funding itself. One whose cash keeps arriving through financing is being funded by someone else, and that can stop.

Recap · 7 / 8

Here's What You Now Know

The whole lesson, in six lines.

✓The balance sheet always balances: assets on one side, liabilities and equity on the other, equal by definition.
✓It is a snapshot of one single day, while the other two statements cover a stretch of time.
✓Equity is an accounting leftover, not a valuation — it is built from what assets cost, not what they are worth.
✓The cash flow statement splits into operating, investing and financing, and the three explain every dollar that moved.
✓Operating cash flow is the honest one: profit is a judgement, cash either arrived or it did not.
✓A ratio like the current ratio raises a question. It never answers one on its own.
Your next move: find one company's operating cash flow and net income side by side. If the two have drifted apart over a few years, you've found something worth understanding.
