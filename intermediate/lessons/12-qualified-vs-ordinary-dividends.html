Myth Or Fact · 6 / 9

Myth Or Fact?

Five claims you'll hear about dividend taxes. Tap your answer.

All dividends from U.S. companies are automatically taxed at the lower qualified rate.

Myth. The company being American doesn't matter if the holding-period rule isn't met. Sell too soon around the ex-dividend date and the same company's dividend stays ordinary.

Qualified dividends are taxed using the same 0%, 15%, and 20% brackets as long-term capital gains.

Fact. They share one rate table. A taxable income under about $49,450 (single, 2026) pays 0% federal tax on either one.

Dividends paid inside a 401(k) or IRA are taxed as either qualified or ordinary the year they're paid.

Myth. The qualified/ordinary split only matters in a taxable account. Inside a tax-deferred or tax-exempt account, nothing is taxed as it's paid at all.

A stock bought the day before the ex-dividend date and sold the next day won't produce a qualified dividend.

Fact. That's roughly two days held — nowhere near the 61-day minimum. The dividend stays ordinary no matter how well-known the company is.

If your income is low enough, you can still owe some federal tax on a qualified dividend.

Myth. Inside the 0% bracket, the federal income tax owed on a qualified dividend is exactly $0 — not a small amount, none.

Recap · 8 / 9

Here's What You Now Know

The whole lesson, in six lines.

✓Ordinary is the default rate for a dividend. Qualified — the lower 0/15/20% rate — has to be earned by meeting the holding-period rule.
✓The rule: hold the stock for more than 60 days within the 121-day window centered on its ex-dividend date.
✓Qualified dividends and long-term capital gains share the same 0%, 15%, and 20% rate table.
✓The qualified/ordinary split only matters in a taxable account — tax-advantaged accounts skip it entirely.
✓At low enough income, the federal tax on a qualified dividend is exactly $0.
✓The dollar gap between the two rates grows with your income — it was $240 at $45,000 and $300 at $600,000 on the same $2,000.
Your next move: pull up last year's 1099-DIV and compare boxes 1a and 1b. If they're not equal, part of what you were paid was ordinary.
