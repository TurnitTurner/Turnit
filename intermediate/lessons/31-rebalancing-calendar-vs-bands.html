Myth Or Fact · 5 / 8

Myth Or Fact?

Five claims. Tap your answer.

Rebalancing more often produces better results.

Myth. Monthly, quarterly and annual all landed within a tenth of a point of each other on return and volatility over eighty years. What rose sharply with frequency was the number of trades and the cost of making them.

Not rebalancing at all produced the highest return in that study.

Fact — 9.1% against 8.6%. It also ended up 84% in stocks with volatility of 14.4%. You earned more because you were taking far more risk than you signed up for, and you would have found that out in a crash.

Rebalancing means selling your best investment, which is backwards.

Myth. It means trimming a position that has grown past the share you decided it should have. You are not betting against it; you are refusing to let one holding quietly become the whole plan.

You can rebalance without selling anything.

Fact. Point new contributions and dividends at whatever is below target. It is slower, and in a taxable account it avoids realizing a gain you did not have to realize.

A tolerance band means you check your portfolio less often.

Myth. It means you trade less often. You still have to look, or you will not know the band has been breached. Checking and acting are two different decisions, and a band separates them.

Your Call · 6 / 8

Band Breached, Or Just Noise?

Target is 60/40 with a 5-point band. Three situations.

Stocks have run up. You are now at 63/37.

Leave it. Three points out is inside the band. The whole point of setting one is that you do not act on every wobble — otherwise you have a calendar method with extra steps.

A long slide in stocks has left you at 52/48.

Rebalance. Eight points out, and on the uncomfortable side — it means buying stocks after they have fallen. This is the moment the rule exists for, and the moment almost nobody wants to follow it.

You are at 61/39, but it is January and you always rebalance in January.

Leave it. A date on the calendar is a prompt to look, not an instruction to trade. One point out is noise, and trading it just donates a spread to the market for no change in risk.

Answered 0 Of 3

Illustrations of how a band works, not guidance on what band to choose. The right answer for a real portfolio depends on account type, costs and your own situation.

Recap · 7 / 8

Here's What You Now Know

The whole lesson, in six lines.

✓Left alone, a 60/40 portfolio drifted to 84% stocks — the return rose, and so did the risk you never agreed to.
✓Monthly, quarterly and annual rebalancing all landed in the same place. The schedule is not where the decision is.
✓A tolerance band cuts the number of trades sharply while leaving return and volatility essentially unchanged.
✓Checking and trading are separate decisions: look on a schedule, act when the band is breached.
✓New contributions and dividends can do the work without selling anything, which matters in a taxable account.
✓Rebalancing is risk control. If you are doing it to make more money, you have the wrong reason.
Your next move: work out what your mix is right now and compare it with what you meant it to be. Most people have not looked in over a year, and are further out than they expect.
