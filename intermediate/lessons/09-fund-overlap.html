Myth Or Fact · 5 / 8

Myth Or Fact?

Five claims about holding more than one fund. Tap your answer.

More funds means more diversification.

Myth. Adding a fund only widens what you own if it holds something new. Three funds that all buy large US companies is one bet bought three times.

Two funds can share few holdings and still overlap heavily.

Fact. This is why weighted overlap matters. What counts is where the money sits, not how many names appear on both lists.

Overlap means you are paying fees twice on the same company.

Myth. Each fund charges only on the money you hold in it, so nothing is billed twice. The cost of overlap is concentration you did not choose, not a double fee.

A total US market fund already contains the entire S&P 500.

Fact. VTI holds the whole US market, so every company in VOO is inside it already. Buying both means part of your money is counted twice.

If two funds overlap, one of them is the wrong fund.

Myth. Overlap is a fact to measure, not a verdict. Holding one of the two deliberately is fine; holding both while believing you are spread across two different things is the actual problem.

Recap · 7 / 8

Here's What You Now Know

The whole lesson, in six lines.

✓Fund names tell you nothing about whether two funds hold the same companies.
✓Weighted overlap is the number that matters — where the money sits, not how many names match.
✓VOO and VTI overlap about 88.6% by weight, because the whole US market already contains the S&P 500.
✓Overlap costs you concentration, not double fees — the position is bigger than it looks.
✓A real complement holds what you don't already own; another large US fund does not.
✓Overlap is a measurement, not a verdict. Holding one deliberately beats holding both by accident.
Your next move: list every fund you own and find their three largest holdings. If the same names keep appearing, you already have your answer.
