Myth Or Fact · 5 / 8

Myth Or Fact?

Five claims you'll hear about risk and behavior. Tap your answer.

A questionnaire filled out in a calm market reliably predicts what someone will actually do during a real crash.

Myth. A questionnaire answer is a stated preference, made with nothing actually at stake. It's a reasonable starting point, not a guarantee of future behavior.

The average investor's actual dollar-weighted return has tended to run behind the time-weighted return the funds they bought actually produced.

Fact. Morningstar's own research has found this gap running roughly 1-1.5 percentage points a year, and persistent across the periods it has measured.

Most people feel the pain of a loss roughly twice as intensely as the pleasure of an equally-sized gain.

Fact. That's loss aversion — a well-documented, roughly two-to-one asymmetry from behavioral-finance research, not just "people don't love losing."

Selling during a downturn and buying back in once it feels safe again is a proven way to improve returns.

Myth. This pattern — selling low, buying back higher once it feels calm — is a leading cause of the behavior gap itself, not a fix for it.

Simply knowing the behavior gap exists is enough, by itself, to guarantee you won't fall into it.

Myth. Awareness helps, but loss aversion operates in the moment, under real pressure — the same pressure that made the gap show up in the data in the first place.

Recap · 7 / 8

Here's What You Now Know

The whole lesson, in six lines.

✓What you'd say on a questionnaire and what you'd actually do under real pressure are often different things.
✓In a real downturn, the decision you actually make is shaped by loss aversion, not just by what you reported beforehand.
✓Morningstar's own research has found investors' actual dollar-weighted returns running roughly 1-1.5 percentage points a year behind the time-weighted returns their funds produced — a persistent gap, not a one-time event.
✓In 2008 alone, investors pulled an estimated $233 billion out of U.S. equity funds — much of it while prices were still falling.
✓The same real decision point produced wildly different dollar outcomes in this lesson's scenario depending only on what someone actually did — selling at the bottom locked in a permanent loss; staying invested, or buying more, didn't.
✓A questionnaire answer written in a calm moment isn't a guarantee — it's a guess, made without the pressure that actually tests it.
Your next move: the next time the market drops, write down what you actually did — not what you would have guessed beforehand. That's the only real risk-tolerance questionnaire there is.
