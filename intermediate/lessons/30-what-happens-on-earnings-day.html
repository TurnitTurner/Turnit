Myth Or Fact · 5 / 8

Myth Or Fact?

Five claims. Tap your answer.

Beating the estimate means the stock goes up.

Myth. Roughly three in four companies beat every quarter, so beating is the normal outcome and is already expected. What moves the price is the gap between the result and what people were really braced for.

Companies have an incentive to set a bar they can clear.

Fact. Management talks to analysts all quarter and nudges expectations toward something comfortably achievable. A beat against a bar the company helped set is a weaker signal than it looks.

The release is where the real information is.

Myth. The release is backward-looking and heavily polished. The earnings call is where management has to answer questions it did not choose, and that is where the useful detail usually comes out.

A miss can be punished far harder than a beat is rewarded.

Fact. When beating is the default, failing to beat says something genuinely new. The asymmetry is part of why holding a single stock through earnings feels so different from holding a fund through the same day.

If you hold index funds, earnings day is something to watch.

Myth. Hundreds of companies report over a few weeks, and in a broad fund their surprises largely offset. Understanding earnings day is worth it so you can recognize the noise — not so you can trade it.

Your Call · 6 / 8

Which Way Does It Go?

Three releases. Pick the most likely reaction.

Beats by 2%, roughly the usual margin. Revenue in line. Guidance unchanged.

Barely moves. Everything landed where it was expected to land, so there is no new information to price. A beat this ordinary is already in the number.

Beats on earnings and revenue. Cuts next quarter's revenue guidance by 8%.

Falls. The quarter being reported is finished and already owned. The guidance is about quarters nobody has priced yet, and an 8% cut rewrites all of them at once.

Misses on earnings because of a one-off legal charge. Raises guidance. Call is upbeat.

Rises. The miss is a one-off that will not repeat, so the market looks straight past it to the raised forecast. This is the mirror image of the low-quality beat.

Answered 0 Of 3

Simplified illustrations of a common pattern. Real reactions depend on what was already priced in, and markets regularly do the opposite of the sensible thing.

Recap · 7 / 8

Here's What You Now Know

The whole lesson, in six lines.

✓Beating the consensus estimate is the normal outcome — roughly three in four companies do it every quarter.
✓Because it is expected, a beat is already in the price. Only the surprise moves the stock.
✓Guidance usually matters more than the result, because the reported quarter is already owned.
✓The reversal often happens during the earnings call, not when the release lands.
✓Where a beat came from matters: a one-off gain and real growth look identical in the headline.
✓In a broad fund, hundreds of these offset each other. Knowing the pattern is for recognizing noise, not trading it.
Your next move: read one earnings call transcript for a company you already own or use. They're free on company investor-relations sites, and it's the fastest way to see how much the release leaves out.
