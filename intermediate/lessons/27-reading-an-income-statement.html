The Margins · 4 / 8

Three Numbers, Three Questions

All three start from the same revenue. The first takes out only cost of sales, the next takes out more, the last takes out everything. Tap each to see which question it settles.

Tap A MarginAll three are the same revenue, measured after different costs come out.

Read 0 Of 3

A margin on its own means little. Gross margin earns meaning next to a direct competitor's; operating income next to the same company a year earlier. Net income is the least comparable of the three, because tax and one-off items differ so much between companies.

Myth Or Fact · 5 / 8

Myth Or Fact?

Five claims about the income statement. Tap your answer.

Rising revenue means a company is doing better.

Myth. A company can grow the top line by discounting, by buying other businesses, or by selling more of something it barely profits on. Revenue up with gross margin down usually means it bought those sales.

Gross margin says more about pricing power than net income does.

Fact. Gross margin compares what customers pay against what the product costs to deliver, and nothing else. Net income has been through tax, financing and one-off items that have nothing to do with pricing.

Net income is cash the company now has.

Myth. It is an accounting result, counting sales that may not be paid for yet and costs that were paid years ago. The next lesson deals with the statement that tracks actual cash.

Earnings per share can rise while net income falls.

Fact. It is a fraction. If the company buys back enough of its own shares, the denominator shrinks faster than the numerator and the per-share figure rises on its own.

A company spending heavily on research must be struggling.

Myth. Research sits in operating expenses, so it does reduce this year's profit — deliberately. It is money spent on products that do not exist yet, which is what a company with a future tends to do.

Recap · 7 / 8

Here's What You Now Know

The whole lesson, in six lines.

✓The statement runs top to bottom: revenue first, every cost in order, what survives at the end.
✓Revenue minus cost of sales gives gross margin — the cleanest read on pricing power.
✓Research and overheads come out next, leaving operating income: profit from running the business.
✓Below that sit financing, one-off items and tax, which is why net income can move when the business hasn't.
✓Earnings per share is a fraction, so buybacks can lift it while profit falls.
✓None of it is cash. That is a different statement, and it comes next.
Your next move: pull up the income statement of a company you actually use and find the three margins. They're on the first page of any annual report.
