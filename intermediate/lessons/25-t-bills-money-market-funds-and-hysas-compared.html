Myth Or Fact · 5 / 8

Myth Or Fact?

Five claims you'll hear about cash and cash-like accounts. Tap your answer.

All savings accounts pay roughly the same interest rate.

Myth. The gap between the national average savings rate and a top high-yield savings account can be ten times over — even though both are ordinary, FDIC-insured bank accounts.

A money market fund's share price is designed to stay at exactly $1.00, but that isn't guaranteed.

Fact. True for most funds sold to individual investors, which aim to hold a stable $1.00 share price — though it isn't guaranteed. Breaking the buck is rare, but it has happened: most famously the Reserve Primary Fund in September 2008, whose share price fell to $0.97 during the financial crisis.

A T-bill's "interest" is really just the gap between the discounted price you pay and the full face value you receive at maturity.

Fact. Same discount-pricing mechanic Lesson 22 covered for bonds generally — a T-bill just has no coupon payments in between, so the entire return is that one gap.

A money market fund is covered by FDIC insurance, just like a bank savings account.

Myth. A money market FUND is a security, not a bank deposit — nothing like FDIC insurance stands behind it if its holdings lose value, which is exactly why breaking the buck is a real, if rare, possibility. (A bank's money market ACCOUNT — a different product with a confusingly similar name — is FDIC-insured just like any other savings account.)

Interest earned on T-bills is generally exempt from state and local income tax, though it's still taxed federally.

Fact. One line worth knowing — the full mechanics of how that's reported belong to Module 3's lessons on tax, not here.

Recap · 7 / 8

Here's What You Now Know

The whole lesson, in six lines.

✓Lesson 01's "cash" bucket is really three different things — Treasury bills, money market funds, and savings accounts — each with its own rate, protection, and catch.
✓A Treasury bill is a direct, fixed-term loan to the U.S. government, sold at a discount instead of paying a coupon; a money market fund is a pooled, continuously-rolling basket that often holds T-bills among other things.
✓FDIC insurance protects bank deposits (up to $250,000) if the bank fails; SIPC protects brokerage accounts (up to $500,000, $250,000 of that for cash) if the brokerage fails — neither protects an investment from simply losing value.
✓A money market fund's $1.00 share price is a design goal, not a guarantee — breaking the buck is rare, but real.
✓The gap between a top high-yield savings account and the national average can be enormous, even though both carry the exact same FDIC protection.
✓Next: Module 6 begins — stepping away from funds entirely to look at individual companies, starting with whether you should pick stocks at all.
Your next move: before parking cash anywhere, check which of these three it actually is. The label on the account tells you less than the rate, the protection, and the term do.
