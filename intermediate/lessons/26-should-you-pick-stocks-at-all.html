Myth Or Fact · 5 / 8

Myth Or Fact?

Five things people believe about picking stocks. Tap your answer.

Most individual stocks beat a savings-style return over their lifetime.

Myth. Over 1926–2016, 57.4% of US stocks returned less than one-month Treasury bills across their whole life. The index rises because of a minority, not a majority.

Picking a good company is not the same as picking a good investment.

Fact. The price already reflects what everyone knows about that good company. You profit only if it does better than the market already expects, and that gap is the edge you would need. It is a different and much harder question.

Enough research reliably closes the gap with the professionals.

Myth. The professionals are the ones failing. Nearly 93% of funds running active management trailed the S&P 500 over twenty years, with full-time due diligence and research teams behind them.

Using a small slice of your portfolio limits what this can cost you.

Fact. A deliberately small sleeve caps the damage and keeps the rest of the plan intact. The trouble starts when picking quietly becomes the main portfolio instead of a corner of it.

A few good calls prove you have an edge.

Myth. In a market that rises more often than it falls, and with return skew meaning a single holding can carry a whole sleeve, a run of winners is what luck looks like. It takes far more decisions than most people ever make to tell skill from chance.

Guardrails · 6 / 8

If You Still Want To

Plenty of people do this anyway, and some have good reasons. Open each guardrail serious active management tends to use.

Keep It A Sleeve, Not The Plan

A fixed, small share of the portfolio — decided in advance and written down — means a bad run costs you a known amount. The core keeps compounding either way, so the opportunity cost stays capped and the experiment never threatens the plan.

Write The Case Before You Buy

One page: what you think will happen, why the price does not already reflect it, and what would prove you wrong. Written beforehand, it is the only way to tell later whether you had an edge or were merely lucky.

Measure Against The Index, Honestly

Compare the whole sleeve — winners, losers and the cash sitting idle — against what the same money would have done in the index. Counting only your best pick is how return skew tricks people into concluding they are good at this.

Decide When You'd Stop

Pick the review date and the result that would make you fold the sleeve back into the index. Deciding that while you are calm is far easier than deciding it after three bad years of due diligence that led nowhere.

Recap · 7 / 8

Here's What You Now Know

The whole lesson, in six lines.

✓Nearly 93% of professional US large-cap funds trailed the S&P 500 over twenty years.
✓Most individual stocks lose to Treasury bills over their lifetime; a little over 4% of companies produced all the net wealth.
✓That return skew means missing the few winners is the normal outcome, not bad luck.
✓A good company is not a good investment unless the price understates it — that gap is your edge, and most people don't have one.
✓Real due diligence costs serious hours a year, and those hours are an opportunity cost nothing on your statement will show.
✓If you do it anyway: a small written-down sleeve, a case written before you buy, and an honest comparison against the index.
Your next move: if you already own individual stocks, add up what the sleeve has returned — all of it, losers included — and compare it with the index over the same stretch.
