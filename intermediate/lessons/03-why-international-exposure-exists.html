Myth Or Fact · 6 / 9

Myth Or Fact?

Four claims about investing abroad. Tap your answer.

Big US companies sell worldwide, so you already have global exposure.

Myth. Foreign revenue is not foreign ownership. Those shares still trade on US exchanges, in dollars, moving with US sentiment — you get some of the economics, none of the diversification.

International stocks and US stocks often fall together in a global panic.

Fact. In a genuine crisis almost everything drops at once, and diversification disappoints exactly when you want it most. The case for holding both is about decades, not about a bad fortnight.

International has lagged for years, so it's a worse investment.

Myth. That is performance chasing with extra steps. A stretch of underperformance tells you what already happened, not what comes next, and leadership has switched more than once.

Holding international means accepting currency movements as part of your return.

Fact. Unless the fund hedges, the exchange rate is part of what you earn — helping in some years and hurting in others.

Recap · 8 / 9

Here's What You Now Know

The whole lesson, in five lines.

✓Roughly a third of the world's listed value sits outside the US — and that share moves over time.
✓Home bias is normal and nearly universal. Noticing it is the point, not curing it.
✓Leadership has switched between US and international more than once, and nobody rings a bell at the turn.
✓Foreign revenue from US companies is not the same as foreign ownership.
✓Currency movement comes along with international holdings — in both directions.
✓There's no single correct international share — 0%, 10–20%, 30–40%, and global market weight are all positions serious investors actually hold.
Your next move: check what share of your stocks is already international. Many broad funds hold none at all, and most people are surprised by the answer.
