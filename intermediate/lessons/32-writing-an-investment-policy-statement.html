Myth Or Fact · 5 / 8

Myth Or Fact?

Five claims. Tap your answer.

An investment policy statement is only worth it for large portfolios.

Myth. The document costs nothing and takes twenty minutes. Its value is behavioral, not financial, and the behavior it guards against — selling at the bottom — does proportionally the same damage at any size.

Writing it down works partly because it is awkward to contradict yourself.

Fact. That is the whole mechanism of a commitment device. An intention you only held in your head can be quietly revised; a sentence you wrote and dated has to be actively overruled, and that friction is the point.

A good policy covers every situation you might face.

Myth. A page that tries to cover everything gets read by nobody, including its author. Cover the handful of decisions you know you will get wrong under stress, and leave the rest to judgement.

The document should say what you will not do, not just what you will.

Fact. Nearly all the damage comes from actions taken, not omitted. A constraint is the part of the page doing the heaviest lifting, because it is the part that speaks when you most want to act.

If the market changes dramatically, you should rewrite your policy.

Myth. A market move is the one thing the document was written in advance to survive. Changes to your life — income, timeline, dependants — are the legitimate reasons, and they belong at your scheduled review date.

Your Call · 6 / 8

What's Missing?

Three real-looking drafts. Each is missing one thing that would matter on a bad day.

"Target 80/20. I will rebalance every January if the mix is more than 5 points out. I will not hold individual stocks."

Why the money exists. There is no purpose or horizon anywhere in it. Without that, nothing in the document can be argued with or defended — an 80/20 mix is sensible for a thirty-year goal and reckless for a two-year one, and the page cannot tell you which it is.

"Retirement in 30 years. Target 90/10. I will check every January and rebalance if 5 points out."

Anything about what you won't do. It describes the portfolio perfectly and says nothing about behavior. The January rule survives a calm year easily; it is the March where stocks are down 30% that needed a sentence, and there isn't one.

"Retirement in 30 years. Target 90/10. I will not sell in a downturn and will not chase individual stocks."

When you will actually act. The purpose, the mix and the constraints are all there, but nothing says when you look or what triggers a trade — so the 90/10 target quietly becomes whatever drift makes it.

Recap · 7 / 8

Here's What You Now Know

The whole lesson, in six lines.

✓An investment policy statement is one page written while calm, to be obeyed while not.
✓Four things earn their place: why the money exists, what you'll hold, when you'll act, and what you refuse to do.
✓It works as a commitment device — a written sentence has to be actively overruled, and that friction is the mechanism.
✓Every line must be falsifiable. If nobody could tell you'd broken it, it is a sentiment, not a policy.
✓The constraint section does the heaviest lifting, because the damage comes from actions taken, not omitted.
✓A market move is never a reason to rewrite it. A change in your own life, at your review date, is.
Your next move: actually write the page — a note on your phone is fine. Date it. The version in your head does not work, and that is the entire finding.
