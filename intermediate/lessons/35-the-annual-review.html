Myth Or Fact · 5 / 8

Myth Or Fact?

Five claims. Tap your answer.

A good annual review means finding something to improve.

Myth. Most years the honest outcome is "nothing to do, raise the contribution rate, carry on." If you come out of every review with changes, the review has become a reason to tinker rather than a check on whether tinkering is needed.

Raising what you pay in matters more than improving what you hold.

Fact, early on and by a wide margin. The gap between a decent portfolio and a perfect one is small. The gap between saving 5% and saving 10% is not, and it is entirely within your control.

If your plan has not changed in five years, you are not paying attention.

Myth. A plan that survives five years without needing a rewrite is a plan that was built properly. Stability is the goal, not evidence of neglect — provided you have actually been looking.

The same change can be a plan change for one person and a reaction for another.

Fact. Moving to more bonds is a reaction if stocks just fell and a sound decision if you just moved your target date five years closer. The action is identical; only the reason differs, and only you know which one you have.

You should review the plan whenever markets are unusually volatile.

Myth, and it is the trap this whole module exists to close. Volatility is the condition the plan was written to survive. Reviewing because of it is how a schedule quietly becomes an excuse.

Your Call · 6 / 8

Change It, Rebalance It, Or Leave It?

Three annual reviews. One of the three answers is used twice.

A strong year has left you at 71/29 against a 60/40 target with a 5-point band.

Rebalance, plan unchanged. Eleven points out is well past the band, so the rule you already wrote says trade. Nothing about the plan itself needs rewriting — this is the plan working exactly as designed.

You've decided to buy a house in three years instead of ten.

Change the plan. The timeline is the thing the whole allocation was built around, and it just moved by seven years. This passes the mirror test easily — it would be just as true if markets had doubled.

Your international fund has lagged US stocks for four years and you're tired of it.

Rebalance, plan unchanged. "Tired of it" is the tell. Lagging is what a diversifier does while the other side runs — and if the band is breached, the rule says buy more of it, which is precisely the trade that feels worst and is hardest to argue against.

Recap · 7 / 8

Here's What You Now Know

The whole lesson, in six lines.

✓The annual review is six questions and about an hour, on a date you set in advance.
✓Most years the right outcome is: raise the contribution rate, rebalance if the band is breached, change nothing else.
✓The mirror test: flip the market the other way. If the reason disappears, it was a reaction.
✓A life event — income, timeline, household, the rules — is what legitimately justifies a plan change.
✓The same action can be sound or foolish depending only on the reason behind it.
✓A plan that goes years without needing a rewrite was built well. That is the whole point of the last thirty-five lessons.
Your next move: put a date in your calendar now, twelve months out, with these six questions in the notes. That single act is most of what this lesson was for.
