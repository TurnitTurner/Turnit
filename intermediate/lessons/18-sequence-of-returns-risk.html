Myth Or Fact · 5 / 8

Myth Or Fact?

Five claims you'll hear about withdrawals and timing. Tap your answer.

Reordering the same sequence of returns only matters for people who are withdrawing from a portfolio, not people who are still contributing to one.

Myth. Contributors are affected just as much — reordering changed a contributor's ending balance by the same amount it changed a withdrawer's, just in the opposite direction. What's unique to withdrawers isn't the sensitivity itself, it's that the sensitivity threatens how long the money actually lasts.

With no money moving in or out of a portfolio, the order those returns arrive in doesn't change its ending balance at all.

Fact. Multiplication doesn't care what order you do it in. It's specifically the act of regularly moving money in or out — withdrawing or contributing — that makes the order start to matter.

A market crash right after you retire is more dangerous to how long your money lasts than the same-size crash ten years into retirement.

Fact. An early crash forces withdrawals from a portfolio that hasn't had time to grow, removing a larger share of what's left — and there's more time left for that damage to compound.

Dollar-cost averaging while contributing and sequence-of-returns risk while withdrawing work in the same direction.

Myth. They're opposites. An early downturn helps a contributor (cheaper shares bought) and hurts a withdrawer (more shares sold at a lower price) — same mechanism, reversed direction.

There's absolutely nothing a retiree can do to reduce sequence-of-returns risk once withdrawals have started.

Myth. It can't be eliminated, but it isn't fixed in stone either — adjusting withdrawal amounts in down years, for instance, is a commonly discussed way people try to reduce its impact.

Recap · 7 / 8

Here's What You Now Know

The whole lesson, in six lines.

✓Sequence-of-returns risk is the risk that the order of returns — not just their average — determines how long a withdrawal portfolio lasts.
✓With no money moving in or out, reordering the exact same returns doesn't change the ending balance at all.
✓During decumulation, a fixed withdrawal forces selling more shares when prices are down — reverse dollar-cost averaging — which is why early losses do outsized damage.
✓During accumulation, the same mechanism works in your favor: an early downturn means buying more shares while they're cheap.
✓The first few years of retirement carry outsized weight — the same crash does more lasting damage early than it does later.
✓The same seven real S&P 500 returns (2000-2006), just reordered, left one simulated account about 17% ahead of the other — same average return, different outcome.
Your next move: if you're near retirement, ask what your plan actually does in a year the market falls 20% right out of the gate — not just what it does on average.
