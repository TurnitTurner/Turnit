What It Means · 2 / 8

You're Buying Years

A P/E ratio of 20 means the price equals twenty years of the company's current profit. Flip the same number over and you get an earnings yield of 5%. Tap a level and count the years.

Tap A LevelForty squares, one per year of today's profit. The ratio decides how many of them you are handing over.

Myth Or Fact · 5 / 8

Myth Or Fact?

Five claims. Tap your answer.

A low P/E means a stock is cheap.

Myth. It means the price is low relative to last year's profit. If that profit is about to halve, the ratio was describing a past that is not coming back. This is how a value trap looks on the day you buy it.

Two companies with the same P/E can deserve very different prices.

Fact. Growth, debt, how reliable the earnings are and how much cash they turn into all belong in the judgement, and none of them appear in the ratio. The number is a starting question.

Comparing P/E ratios across industries tells you which is better value.

Myth. Software and supermarkets have traded at persistently different multiples for decades, for structural reasons. Comparing a company with its own history and its direct competitors is the only comparison that holds.

Plenty of companies have no P/E at all.

Fact. A company that loses money has no meaningful ratio — you cannot divide by a negative and get something you can rank. Apollo's chief economist has put the share of Russell 2000 companies with no earnings at around 40%.

Forward P/E is more reliable because it looks ahead.

Myth. It is more relevant, which is not the same thing. It is built on analysts' estimates, and estimates for the year ahead are revised constantly. Trailing figures are stale but real; forward ones are current but guessed.

Your Call · 6 / 8

Cheap, Expensive, Or Not Enough To Say?

Four companies. One of the three answers is used more than once.

P/E of 7. Most of last year's profit came from selling a warehouse.

Can't tell. Strip out the warehouse and you do not know what the real earnings are yet. The ratio is built on a number that will not repeat, so it is not describing the business at all.

P/E of 45. Profits have fallen three years running, with no turnaround in sight.

Expensive. A high multiple is a bet on growth. Here the earnings are going the other way, so you are paying a growth price for a shrinking business.

P/E of 12 for a regulated utility, earnings flat and dependable for a decade.

Cheap, relatively. Dependable earnings are exactly the case where the ratio means what it says, and 12 sits below the long-run market average of about 16. Still worth checking the debt before you agree with yourself.

No P/E shown at all. The company lost money last year.

Can't tell. Not from this. A loss-making young company and a loss-making dying one look identical here, and the ratio has nothing to say about either. You need the cash flow statement and the reason for the loss.

Recap · 7 / 8

Here's What You Now Know

The whole lesson, in six lines.

✓A P/E ratio is years of today's profit — a price tag expressed in time.
✓The long-run market average is about 16, which is an anchor to think against, not a fair value.
✓The bottom of the fraction is fragile: one-off gains, write-offs and buybacks all move it without the business changing.
✓Earnings yield and price-to-book are blind in different directions, which is why nobody sensible uses one ratio alone.
✓Ratios only compare honestly within an industry, and against a company's own history.
✓"Can't tell yet" is a real answer, and usually the correct one.
Your next move: find a company with a surprisingly low P/E and work out why. Either you'll find the catch, or you'll have learned how to check for one.
