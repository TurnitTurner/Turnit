Myth Or Fact · 5 / 8

Myth Or Fact?

Five claims you'll hear about bonds and bond funds. Tap your answer.

If you hold an individual bond to maturity, rising interest rates never cost you anything.

Myth. You do get face value back at maturity, assuming no default — pull to par genuinely erases the price drop itself by then. What it doesn't erase: the below-market income you earned holding an older, lower-paying bond while new ones paid more, and the real, locked-in loss you'd have taken by selling before maturity.

A bond fund with a similar duration to an individual bond goes through a similar total-return recovery after a single rate shock — it just doesn't arrive via one fixed maturity date.

Fact. Reinvesting at the new, higher yield does the same work for a fund that pulling to par does for a single bond — it's duration, not a maturity date, that drives the timing.

A bond fund's yield is locked in when you buy it, the same way an individual bond's purchase yield is.

Myth. A fund's yield reflects whatever bonds it currently holds, which keeps changing as old bonds roll off and new ones are bought — there's no single number locked in at your purchase the way there is with one bond.

A bond ladder's staggered maturities give you a predictable, recurring reinvestment schedule without needing a fund at all.

Fact. Each rung matures on its own date, handing you cash to spend or reinvest at whatever rates exist then — spread out instead of concentrated on one day.

A defined-maturity fund is a real type of bond fund built with a fixed end date, and it does pull to par like a single bond.

Fact. These are the exception — most bond funds have no maturity date of their own, but this category is specifically built to wind down like one bond would.

Recap · 7 / 8

Here's What You Now Know

The whole lesson, in six lines.

✓An individual bond held to maturity returns exactly its face value on a known date, assuming no default — pull to par genuinely erases the price drop by then. It doesn't erase the below-market income earned along the way, or the real loss you'd have locked in by selling early.
✓A bond fund with a similar duration goes through a similar total-return recovery after a single rate shock — it just arrives through rising reinvestment income rather than one fixed maturity date.
✓Most bond funds never "mature" the way a single bond does. Defined-maturity funds are the real, named exception, built to wind down and pull to par on a set date.
✓An individual bond concentrates you in one issuer's credit risk; a bond fund spreads that risk across many issuers, usually with far less capital than buying several bonds yourself.
✓A bond ladder — several individual bonds with staggered maturities — gives you a predictable reinvestment schedule without needing a fund at all.
✓Next: unpacking "cash" itself — T-bills, money market funds, and high-yield savings accounts, and what actually backs each one.
Your next move: before choosing between a bond and a bond fund, ask what date you actually need the money. A fixed date points toward a bond, a ladder, or a defined-maturity fund; no fixed date points toward an ordinary fund.
