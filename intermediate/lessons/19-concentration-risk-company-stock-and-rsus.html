Myth Or Fact · 5 / 8

Myth Or Fact?

Five claims you'll hear about employer stock. Tap your answer.

If your company offers a discount on its stock through an ESPP, there's no real downside to buying as much as you can.

Myth. The discount is real money, but holding the shares afterward still adds to single-stock exposure, on top of whatever your paycheck already riding on the same company.

Owning a lot of employer stock creates a different kind of risk than owning a lot of any other single stock, because your job is also on the line.

Fact. That's double exposure: the same event can hit your paycheck and your portfolio at once, something an ordinary single-stock bet doesn't do.

RSUs are a fixed, guaranteed dollar amount, like a cash bonus.

Myth. An RSU is a promise of shares, not cash — once it vests, it's simply worth whatever the stock is worth at that moment, which can be more or less than expected.

A company's own employees are sometimes among the last to find out when something is seriously wrong.

Fact. Multiple former Enron employees testified before Congress that they were encouraged to keep holding company stock without being told how serious the company's problems already were.

There's a standard, official rule for the maximum percentage of your portfolio that should ever be in employer stock.

Myth. No single universal rule exists. It depends on your own job security, savings elsewhere, and how much risk you can tolerate — which is exactly why it's worth deliberately tracking the number, not just sensing it.

Recap · 7 / 8

Here's What You Now Know

The whole lesson, in six lines.

✓Concentration risk is the risk of having too much wealth tied to a single company, however that concentration built up.
✓Owning your own employer's stock creates double exposure: your paycheck and your portfolio can both be hit by the same event, at the same time.
✓Your human capital — your own future earning power — already functions like an asset tied to your employer, even before you own a single share of its stock.
✓Concentration from employer stock usually builds gradually, from several ordinary benefits, rather than one big decision.
✓At Enron, Senate testimony found the typical employee's 401(k) held more than half its assets in company stock — stock that collapsed the same month about 4,000 employees lost their jobs.
✓There's no single official rule for how much employer stock is "too much" — it depends on your situation, but it's worth knowing the actual number.
Your next move: add up every form of employer stock you hold — RSUs, ESPP, 401(k) match — as a percentage of your whole portfolio. Most people have never actually added it up.
